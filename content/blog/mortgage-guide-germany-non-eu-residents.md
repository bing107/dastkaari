Buying property in Germany as a Pakistani is legally straightforward - there are no restrictions on foreigners owning real estate here. The harder part is financing it. Getting a mortgage as a non-EU resident takes more preparation than a German citizen would need, but it's absolutely possible. Thousands of expats do it every year.

This guide focuses on the mortgage side of the equation. If you want the broader property-buying picture first, read our [guide to buying property in Germany as a Pakistani expat](/blog/buy-property-germany-pakistani-expat-guide/). This article goes deeper on financing - eligibility, banks, documents, and process.

## What German Banks Actually Care About

German banks are conservative lenders. They want to know three things before approving a mortgage:

1. Can you repay the loan from stable, ongoing income?
2. How long will you legally stay in Germany?
3. What happens to the property if you leave?

For German citizens, questions two and three are non-issues. For non-EU residents, they are central to every credit decision. A bank giving you a 30-year mortgage needs to believe you'll still be in Germany (or at least legally obligated to repay) for the full term. A short-term work permit makes that harder to argue.

The good news is that German banks are not hostile to foreign borrowers - they just need more documentation and will typically require a larger down payment to offset their perceived risk.

## How Your Residency Status Affects What You Can Borrow

Your residence permit type is the single biggest variable in mortgage approval. Here's how it generally maps out:

| Residency Status | Typical Max LTV | Required Down Payment | Outlook |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Niederlassungserlaubnis** (permanent residency) | 80-90% | 10-20% | Very good, near-German terms |
| **EU Blue Card** (2+ years, stable income) | 70-80% | 20-30% | Good, most banks will lend |
| **EU Blue Card** (new, under 12 months) | 60-70% | 30-40% | Manageable, expect more scrutiny |
| **Standard work permit** | 50-60% | 40-50% | Challenging, fewer lenders |
| **Non-resident (living abroad)** | 40-50% | 50-60% | Very difficult, specialist banks only |

<!-- FOUNDER-VERIFY: These LTV ranges reflect general market norms as of mid-2026. Confirm with a current mortgage broker like Hypofriend or Interhyp that these ranges still hold, as credit conditions can shift. -->

LTV stands for Loan-to-Value - the percentage of the property's purchase price (or bank valuation, whichever is lower) that the bank will lend. If a bank offers 70% LTV on a €400,000 apartment, you need at least €120,000 in cash for the down payment, plus the purchase costs on top.

The practical takeaway for Pakistanis on a [Blue Card](/blog/germany-blue-card-guide-pakistanis/): the longer you've been employed in Germany and the more stable your income history, the better your terms will be. If you can wait until you hold a Niederlassungserlaubnis before buying, you'll get significantly better financing.

## Down Payment Requirements

The minimum down payment in Germany for any buyer is typically 20% of the purchase price. For non-EU residents, plan for 25-40% depending on your permit type and bank.

But that's not the full cash requirement. Germany has significant purchase costs (called Nebenkosten) that the bank does not finance. You pay these on top of the down payment:

| Cost | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Grunderwerbsteuer** (property transfer tax) | 3.5% - 6.5% of purchase price | Varies by state; Bavaria is 3.5%, Berlin is 6% |
| **Notarkosten** (notary fees) | ~1.5-2% | Mandatory for all property transactions in Germany |
| **Grundbucheintragung** (land registry) | ~0.5% | Registering the new ownership |
| **Maklerprovision** (estate agent fee) | 0-3.57% (buyer's share) | Since 2020, seller and buyer share agent fees |

<!-- FOUNDER-VERIFY: Confirm current Grunderwerbsteuer rates by state, as these are set by state governments and can be adjusted. Berlin was 6% as of 2026-03 but has discussed changes. -->

A realistic total: on a €350,000 property in Berlin, budget €350,000 (purchase) plus roughly €35,000 in Nebenkosten (10%), then your down payment on top of that. If you're putting 30% down, that's €105,000 in a down payment plus €35,000 in costs - €140,000 in cash needed before the bank funds anything.

This is why saving before you buy is so important. The Nebenkosten alone can wipe out a buyer who planned for only the down payment.

## Key Documents You'll Need

German banks want a complete picture before they'll even give you a preliminary offer. Start collecting these well before you apply:

**Income documents:**
- Last 3 payslips (Lohnabrechnungen)
- Most recent tax assessment (Steuerbescheid) if you have one
- Employment contract (Arbeitsvertrag), especially if it's permanent (unbefristet)
- Bank statements from the last 3-6 months

**Identity and residency:**
- Valid passport
- Current residence permit (Aufenthaltstitel)
- Anmeldebescheinigung (registration certificate from your Einwohnermeldeamt)

**Property documents:**
- Exposé (property listing) with floor plan (Grundriss)
- Grundbuchauszug (land registry extract) - the seller or agent provides this
- Energy certificate (Energieausweis)
- Building plans if it's a new build

**Financial position:**
- Schufa-Auskunft (German credit report - request yours free at Schufa.de once a year)
- Evidence of your down payment savings (account statements)
- Documentation of any existing liabilities (other loans, credit cards)

A clean Schufa is important. If you've been in Germany for less than a year, you might have a thin Schufa file rather than a bad one - which is different. Banks understand this for recent immigrants. ## German Mortgage Types Explained

Before you talk to a bank, know the product you're asking for:

**Annuitätendarlehen (annuity mortgage):** The standard German mortgage. You pay a fixed monthly rate (Annuität) that combines interest and principal repayment. The interest rate is fixed for a defined period - commonly 10, 15, or 20 years. After the fixed period ends, you either negotiate a new rate or switch banks. Most German buyers choose this.

**Tilgungsrate (repayment rate):** You choose your initial repayment percentage (typically 1-3% of the loan per year). Higher repayment means paying off faster and less interest overall. Banks require a minimum, usually around 1-2%.

**Volltilgerdarlehen:** A mortgage where the full loan is paid off within the fixed-interest period. Monthly payments are higher but you have certainty - no refinancing risk at renewal.

For most Pakistani expats buying a primary residence, the standard Annuitätendarlehen with a 10 or 15-year fixed rate is the right starting point. Locking in a long fixed period protects you if rates rise, which matters given how much rates have moved in recent years.

## The Application Process: Step by Step

1. **Check your Schufa** - request your free annual report at schufa.de. Fix any errors before applying.

2. **Calculate your budget** - use a mortgage calculator (Interhyp and Hypofriend both have English-language ones) to understand what monthly payment you can sustain. A common rule of thumb is that your housing costs should not exceed 30-35% of your net income.

3. **Get a Finanzierungsbestätigung** - this is a preliminary financing confirmation from a bank or broker, not a full commitment, but it shows sellers you're a serious buyer. You'll need one before most sellers take you seriously.

4. **Find a property and make an offer** - once you have a target, the seller will want to see your Finanzierungsbestätigung. Negotiate the price.

5. **Submit your full mortgage application** - your broker or bank will request all the documents listed above. The bank orders its own property valuation (Wertgutachten).

6. **Receive your mortgage offer** - typically takes 2-6 weeks. Review it carefully, especially the Sollzinsbindung (fixed rate period), Tilgungsrate, and any special repayment terms.

7. **Sign at the notary** - German property purchases require a notary. The Kaufvertrag (purchase contract) and mortgage documents are signed here. The notary then handles land registry registration.

8. **Funds transfer and key handover** - once the land registry has registered the transfer, the notary releases payment to the seller and you get the keys.

## KfW Loans: Worth Asking About

The KfW (Kreditanstalt für Wiederaufbau) is Germany's state development bank. It offers subsidized loans for energy-efficient homes and certain buyer groups, typically channeled through your main bank rather than directly.

As a non-EU resident with a work permit, you may qualify for some KfW programs, particularly if you're buying or renovating an energy-efficient property. These can reduce your effective interest rate by 0.5-1% on part of the loan, which adds up significantly over a 20-year term.

Ask your mortgage broker specifically about KfW programs - not all brokers proactively suggest them. We'll cover KfW in detail in a dedicated guide.

## Tips to Improve Your Approval Chances

A few things that genuinely move the needle for non-EU applicants:

**Have a permanent employment contract.** An unbefristeter Arbeitsvertrag (permanent, not fixed-term) is a major positive signal. If you're still in your probation period (Probezeit), wait until it ends before applying.

**Show 6-12 months of German payslips.** Even if you technically qualify earlier, banks feel more confident when they can see a track record in Germany.

**Build your Schufa profile first.** This takes time. A bank account, a mobile phone contract, and a credit card used responsibly all help. Read our [settling-in checklist](/blog/first-30-days-germany-checklist-pakistanis/) for what to set up when you arrive.

## The Bottom Line

Getting a mortgage in Germany as a non-EU resident is more demanding than it would be for a German citizen, but it's not unusual. Banks here deal with expats regularly, particularly in cities like Berlin, Munich, and Frankfurt.

The main requirements: a stable German income, enough cash for a 25-40% down payment plus Nebenkosten, a clean Schufa, and the right residence permit. Blue Card holders with 2+ years in Germany are in a genuinely workable position. If you're earlier in your residency, it may be worth waiting and saving until your status strengthens.
